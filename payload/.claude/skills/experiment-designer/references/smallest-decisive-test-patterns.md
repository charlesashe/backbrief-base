# Smallest Decisive Test Patterns

A working list of test shapes, ordered roughly from cheapest to most involved. Use the cheapest pattern that still produces a decisive result for the hypothesis at hand; do not reach for a build when a smaller pattern would answer the same question.

For each pattern: what it tests well, what it does not test, and a worked stop-rule example.

## Direct outreach to a qualified list

Tests: whether a named, reachable segment responds to a specific offer or message, without any product built.
Does not test: whether they will pay, or whether the product can be delivered at the promised quality.
Worked stop rule: "If fewer than 3 of 50 contacted owners reply within two weeks, the message or the list is wrong for this offer; do not repeat this exact message to this exact list before changing one variable and retesting."

## Landing page with a real commitment step

Tests: whether the stated value proposition converts strangers into an action that costs them something, real payment, a deposit, a signed waitlist with a follow-up call scheduled, not just an email address.
Does not test: retention, delivery quality, or whether visitors who convert are the right buyer.
Worked stop rule: "If fewer than 8% of 200 targeted visitors take the paid or scheduled action, the offer as stated does not clear the bar to build; do not swap only the headline and rerun without changing the underlying offer."

## Concierge or manual-first pilot

Tests: whether the outcome, delivered entirely by hand behind an automated-looking front end, is something a real customer will pay for and value, before any of it is built or automated.
Does not test: whether the process can scale or hold margin once automated.
Worked stop rule: "If none of the first 5 manually delivered pilots result in a second paid request, the outcome itself is not wanted at this price; do not proceed to automating delivery."

## Pre-sell or paid waitlist

Tests: real willingness to pay ahead of delivery, the strongest signal available short of shipping.
Does not test: satisfaction after delivery; a refund clause is not optional if this pattern is used.
Worked stop rule: "If fewer than 5 of 100 warm contacts pre-pay within the two-week window, treat the price or the offer, not the audience, as unproven; do not extend the window before revising one of the two."

## Fake-door or interest signal

Tests: raw interest in a concept, cheapest available signal.
Does not test: willingness to pay, or fit with the real buyer; use this only when a stronger pattern above is genuinely not reachable yet, and weight the result accordingly in the brief's evidence tagging (an interest signal is closer to unverified than to a real commitment).
Worked stop rule: "If click-through on the interest prompt is below 2% of 500 qualified impressions, there is not enough raw interest to justify a stronger, costlier test next; stop before spending on a landing page or pre-sell for this exact concept."

## Small paid ad test with a hard spend cap

Tests: whether paid channels can reach the target segment at a workable cost, once a weaker pattern above has already shown interest or intent.
Does not test: organic or outreach channel performance; a result here says nothing about those.
Worked stop rule: "If cost per qualified signal exceeds [the plan's stated acceptable acquisition cost] before the spend cap is reached, this channel is not viable at this price point; do not raise the cap to chase the metric, stop and report the actual cost observed."

## Choosing among patterns

Match the pattern to what the hypothesis actually claims. A hypothesis about willingness to pay needs a pattern with a real payment step; a fake-door result cannot answer it, however good the click-through looks. A hypothesis about channel reach needs a channel test; a landing page test alone cannot answer it. Naming the wrong pattern against the hypothesis is the most common way an experiment brief looks rigorous and answers nothing.
